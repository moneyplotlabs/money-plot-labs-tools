More Parameters
Added to milestone spending while active. Use a negative amount for a spending cut.
Quality of Life Scoring

Scores each run by the spending it actually delivers: planned spending while the money lasts, then only Social Security / windfalls once it runs out. By default your plan's spending is the goal: falling short of it hurts a lot, going over it helps a little. The result is the steady yearly spend that would feel just as good — in today's $.

With mortality on, Life Expectancy acts as the planning horizon — set it to 95–100+ so few runs outlive it.

What these settings do

None of these settings change the simulated markets, or what the Fixed, Constant %, Amortize and Guardrails strategies spend — those follow their own rules, and these settings only change how their years are scored. The Optimal strategy is re-solved to maximize the score, so for it these settings also change what it spends.

Happiness Model: Target (default)
Your target is your plan's spending in each year: the milestone's lifestyle spending plus recurring expenses (working-year or retired-year rows, whichever applies). Spending exactly the target scores 100%. Each year's happiness compares what the run spends with that year's target, so a plan whose spending changes by life stage is judged stage by stage. The steady-spend result is shown in dollars at your average target and as "% of plan". Optimal strategies: the target is a strong anchor — they hold spending at the plan whenever the money allows, cut only when they must, and rise above it only with a real surplus.
Shortfall Pain (×)
Just below the target, each dollar short hurts this many times as much as a dollar over feels good (2.5 = a $1,000 cut hurts like a $2,500 raise helps), and deeper cuts hurt faster still, steeply as you near the floor. Most people land around 2–2.5. The line above the help shows what your setting means at your plan's spending — e.g. how big a raise it takes to make up for a 10% cut. Higher values make every strategy's lean years cost more, and make the optimal strategies hold a bigger cushion to avoid cuts.
Extra Spending Worth
How much spending above the target adds. High: steady diminishing returns (each doubling adds the same). Moderate: saturates — spending twice the target gets you half of all the extra happiness there is. Low: saturates fast; a little above the target is nearly as good as a lot. Lower values make good markets count for less in the score and make the optimal strategies less eager to spend windfalls.
Leftover $ Worth (%)
What money left at death (or at the Life Expectancy horizon) is worth, as a share of a dollar of extra spending at your target: at 25%, the first $4 left are as good as spending $1 more at your plan. Its value tapers slowly as the pile grows — with Moderate extra-spending worth, a leftover dollar keeps at least half its value until you'd leave about 40 years of planned spending — so a huge estate can't dominate the score. Raise it toward 50% to keep spending close to the plan even when there's a surplus. At 0, leftover money is wasted, so the optimal strategies eventually spend any surplus and the score ignores what's left; above 0, they hold spending near the target instead of raising it, and runs that end with money left score a bit higher. (Separate from the Desired Legacy Floor, which only sets the success-rate threshold.)
Swing Aversion (γ) — smooth-curve model only
The alternative model has no target: how fast extra spending stops adding happiness: each year's happiness is (spending − floor)1−γ/(1−γ). Higher γ makes a lean year cost far more than a rich year adds back, so uneven or risky spending scores lower. Across runs, the steady-spend figure sits further below the average outcome. A 50/50 shot at $50k or $100k above the floor feels like a sure $70.7k at γ = 1, $66.7k at γ = 2 and $60.6k at γ = 4. Optimal strategies: γ sets how hard they work to keep spending even. With a meaningful floor, a higher γ makes them hold back a bigger cushion — e.g. at 55 with $600k, a $20k floor and $24k Social Security from 67, about $41k/yr at γ = 2 but $34k at γ = 4. With no floor the effect is small and can go either way, because a higher γ also makes them less willing to put off spending to chase returns.
Spending Floor ($)
The bare minimum. Happiness is measured on spending above the floor, so as spending approaches it the penalty climbs steeply. Below floor + $1,000 the penalty keeps growing in a steep straight line (finite, so one bad year can't swamp everything). Years near or below the floor dominate the score, so a Fixed plan that runs out of money and falls back to Social Security scores close to the floor. The floor is not income: it doesn't put money in, it only sets where "lean" starts. Optimal strategies: they hold back enough to avoid ever dropping toward the floor — for example, they won't leave a gap of near-zero spending before Social Security starts.
Mortality
Weights each year by the chance you're alive to enjoy it, from the CDC/NCHS United States Life Tables, 2023 (Unisex averages the male and female rates; sources under Data Attribution below). Later years count for less, so spending earlier is worth more and running short at 90 hurts less than at 70. Off (the default) treats you as certain to live exactly to the Life Expectancy age. Years past Life Expectancy are never scored; when more than 5% of people would still be alive then, the Quality of Life card says "% outlive horizon". Optimal strategies: with mortality on they spend more early and less late; with it off they spread spending more evenly up to the horizon.
Time Discount
How much less a year of happiness counts for each year further in the future: at d%/yr, a year t years away counts (1 + d)−t as much as this year. It weighs against the return Optimal is solved for: if returns beat the discount, waiting pays and Optimal lets spending rise over time; if the discount beats returns, spending falls. Match expected return (the default) sets d to the mean return of the model Optimal is solved against (shown under the inputs), so Optimal aims for level spending — like Amortization with a well-chosen return — while still holding a cushion for bad markets. Custom uses your own d: below the return to back-load spending, above it to front-load. The discount also weights years in the score, so distant years count less in the steady-spend figure.
Enjoyment at 90 / Declines From Age
How much a year of spending is enjoyed late in life, relative to now: full until the "declines from" age, then a straight line down to the age-90 value, which then holds. 100% (the default) means no decline. Lower values make late years count less in the score and let Optimal spend more earlier.

Methodology: Two simulation modes are available. Bootstrap resampling (60/40, Equities, Manual): runs 1,000 Monte Carlo iterations sampling returns randomly with replacement. Chronological cohort: walks every valid overlapping window through the actual 1928–2024 return sequence — e.g. a 40-year horizon yields 58 cohorts (1928–1967, 1929–1968, … 1985–2024). If both phases use a chronological method (or are linked), the full planning horizon must fit within 97 years; if only one phase is chronological, only that phase's length is constrained. Horizons exceeding 97 years show a warning. Note: Cohort success rates are not directly comparable to bootstrap success rates. Over long horizons, all cohort windows have similar CAGRs (e.g. 4.6–5.9% for 60/40 over 67 years) because sequence variance averages out. Bootstrap can draw pathological sequences that never occurred in real history, producing wider variance and lower success rates. Both are valid but measure different things: cohort = "did any real historical investor fail?"; bootstrap = "stress-testing against synthetic extremes." Quality of life: each run's realized spending (capped at Social Security and windfalls once the portfolio is empty) is scored against that year's planned spending — shortfalls hurt more than the same surplus helps, steeply near the spending floor (or, optionally, with a smooth CRRA curve) — weighted by the chance of being alive each year (CDC 2023 US life tables), an enjoyment-by-age weight and a time discount (by default matched to the expected return). Averaging across runs and converting back to dollars gives the steady-spend equivalent; the 10–90% range is the same figure per run, and "short of plan" is the expected number of years (counting only years you are alive) the run could not fund planned spending. Spending strategies: Fixed spends the milestone plan regardless of markets; Constant % withdraws a share of the current balance plus Social Security; Amortize (VPW) re-solves each year for the level spending that exhausts the balance plus the present value of future Social Security and windfalls by the horizon at an assumed real return; Guardrails (simplified Guyton–Klinger, real terms) scales the plan up or down when the withdrawal rate leaves a band around its starting value. Flexible strategies never borrow. All five run on the same return sequences, so their scores differ only by the strategy. Optimal is the spending policy that maximizes the Quality of Life score itself, solved by backward induction over age and cash on hand (a 300-point wealth grid) against the decumulation return distribution — the exact historical pool for bootstrap methods, a 9-point Gauss–Hermite normal for manual inputs, and the historical pool treated as independent years for chronological cohorts. It knows it cannot borrow against future Social Security, which is where amortization falls short. Solve Optimal Against can instead solve it for a different return model (60/40 or equities history, or a normal with your mean and std dev) while the simulated markets stay as set — a test of how the policy holds up when its return assumptions are wrong, as amortization's assumed return can be. The net worth chart displays a percentile fan: p10/p25 as dashed lines, p50 as the solid median, plus the single actual run at the 10th percentile of outcomes (orange) — for Fixed, ranked by how early the money runs out and then by ending balance; for the flexible strategies, by quality of life — whose growth sequence also drives the cash flow bars. Balances never go below $0: once a run's money is gone, it can only spend what comes in that year (Social Security, windfalls, pay). Success rate = share of runs that never run out and end at or above the legacy floor.

Data Attribution: All historical return series cover 1928–2024 (97 years) and are expressed as real (inflation-adjusted) annual returns.

US Equities (S&P 500) — nominal total returns (price + dividends) sourced from Aswath Damodaran, NYU Stern. CPI deflation applied using U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics data via BLS / Robert Shiller. Real arithmetic mean 8.8%, std dev 19.6%, geometric CAGR 6.9%.

60/40 Portfolio (60% S&P 500 + 40% 10-Year US Treasury Bond) — bond total returns (coupon + price appreciation) from FRED, St. Louis Fed as compiled by Damodaran; blended and deflated by CPI. Real arithmetic mean 5.9%, std dev 12.5%, geometric CAGR 5.1%.

Mortality (life expectancy) — one-year death probabilities by age from the CDC/NCHS United States Life Tables, 2023 (National Vital Statistics Reports Vol. 74, No. 6), using the male (Table 2) and female (Table 3) data files; Unisex averages the two. The tables end in an open "100 and older" group, so ages 100–119 are extended with a Gompertz curve fitted to ages 90–99. Life expectancy at 65: male 18.2, female 20.7 years. More editions: CDC Life Tables.
